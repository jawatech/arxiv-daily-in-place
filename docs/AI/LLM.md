##### **Latent Lattice Language Models**
2611.21608v1 by Shivam Duggal, Zongze Wu, William T. Freeman, Antonio Torralba, Richard Zhang, Eli Shechtman

Language models typically increase per-token computation through deeper or looped layers and latent reasoning steps, while richer residual connections improve information flow across layers. We instead increase computational capacity: the number of active computational states a model maintains. We introduce the Lattice of Latents, a set of per-layer horizontal latent streams that run recurrently along time, alongside the vertical residual stream that flows through depth. Like hyper-connections, mHC, and attention or value residuals, the Lattice enhances the residual stream, but rather than adding or rerouting residual pathways through depth, it provides companion computational states that persist across tokens. Unlike hybrid Transformer-SSM architectures, whose recurrent states primarily serve as internal memory for sequence mixing, these latents directly condition the vertical stream's attention, KV-cache writes, MLP computation, and decoding. On Transformers trained on 20B FineWeb-Edu tokens, a 406M-parameter Lattice model lowers validation perplexity from 15.41 to 14.40 over a parameter-matched Transformer. These gains extend across architectural variants. The Lattice composes with prior residual-stream enhancements: with hyper-connections and input residuals, it lowers validation perplexity from 15.41 (base Transformer) and 14.22 (Transformer with the same enhancements) to 13.61. On Gated DeltaNet, Mamba-2, Triadic GDN, and GDN-Mamba-2 hybrids, it improves perplexity at every tested native state size, with Lattice-GDN and Lattice-Triadic-GDN variants outperforming pure baselines with 4x larger native states. These results identify persistent per-layer state as a complementary axis for scaling language-model computation.

##### **ViRA: Toward Ultra-High-Resolution Visual Agents via Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization**
2611.21605v1 by Huawei Lin, Jinting Liu, Tongyan Wang, Xuyang Chen, Ruihua Shi, Zhichao Xu, Jiaxin Qin, Guoshenghui Zhao, Baijian Yang, Yingjie Victor Chen, Tan Yu, Weijie Zhao

Visual agents answer questions about ultra-high-resolution photos by zooming into the relevant regions. However, existing agents often answer from common sense instead of reading the evidence. Reinforcement learning (RL) on the final answer cannot correct this, because a guess earns the same reward as reading. We propose Cross-Input Relative Policy Optimization (CrossRPO), which trains agents on contrast sets. A contrast set asks one question about several versions of an image that differ only in a small region with the evidence and have different answers, so only reading the evidence solves the whole set. CrossRPO rewards solving the whole set, and we prove that it does not reinforce a set answered from common sense. We build contrast sets for training (ViRA-Train) and evaluation (ViRA-Bench) by editing one small object in images of 21 to 120 megapixels, and we photograph further sets with real changes (ViRA-Bench-Real) to exclude editing traces. We train two Visual Reasoning Agents, ViRA-9B and ViRA-27B, with CrossRPO on only about 3,500 items of ViRA-Train each. CrossRPO achieves 63.3% set accuracy on ViRA-Bench at the maximum resolution, against 59.3% for GRPO. ViRA-9B is the most accurate open model of its size on all benchmarks, and ViRA-27B outperforms Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6-Luna, both at their highest thinking level, on most benchmarks.

##### **Quantifying the added value of human forecaster discussions for tropical cyclone intensity prediction**
2611.21602v1 by Ilia Azizi, Frederick Iat-Hin Tam, Marc-Olivier Boldi, Valérie Chavez-Demoulin, Tom Beucler

The National Hurricane Center routinely releases Tropical Cyclone Discussions that offer insights into its tropical cyclone forecasts. This study examines the added value of these discussions for intensity forecasting beyond a set of numerical predictors. For 637 Atlantic and eastern North Pacific tropical cyclones from the 2002-2023 hurricane seasons, we analyze the discussion text and its combination with 13 hurricane environmental predictors from SHIPS and ERA5 10-meter wind fields, for forecasting intensity changes at lead times of 24 to 120 hours using three modeling methods. In one approach, an end-to-end deep learning framework for the text shows that the discussions alone offer comparable value for 24-hour intensity forecasts to the SHIPS predictors. ##### **Does the Voice Matter? Evaluating Frustration Detection on Deployed Clinical Voice-Agent Calls**
2611.21001v1 by Annika Michael, Zachary Ellis, Yuxiang Zhou, Samuel Thio, Yajie Vera He, Spencer Hazel, Jared Joselowitz

Autonomous voice agents now conduct routine clinical follow-up calls, so some automated triage is unavoidable. A patient becoming frustrated is one thing that triage should catch. These systems typically read only the transcript the speech-to-text stage produces, not the voice, and whether that is enough is largely untested on real clinical speech. We study per-turn frustration detection on real patient calls from a deployed clinical voice agent: 150 turns from six calls labelled by three annotators, with a length-matched acted corpus as a control, and 714 turns from 30 calls. Patient turns are overwhelmingly short: across the deployed calls, roughly 59% are one or two words, against 13% in the full acted corpus, and a corpus accuracy gap remains even after the acted set is length-matched. On one-word turns, transcript-only detection does not exclude chance. Across five audio-capable language models, the transcript flags 3 or 4 of the 39 frustrated one-word turns in the 30 calls, and a classifier given only the word count reaches 57.3%. Whether audio helps depends on the model and on the instruction. Asked to attend to delivery, two Gemini 2.5 models gain 3.0 pp over the transcript across 30 calls (-1.0 to 7.5 pp). Of the turns they confidently clear, 6.9% were in fact frustrated for audio against 13.0% for the transcript, a difference of 6.1 pp (2.2 to 10.4 pp). Across the 30 calls, given one prompt identical for both inputs, no model's audio-text interval excludes zero. Under the input-specific prompts, a newer Gemini model and two smaller open models do worse with audio than with the transcript (-3.5, -8.6 and -10.6 pp). A WavLM probe trained on the clinical calls matches the best prompted model overall (71.6%) but misses every frustrated one-word turn in the 24 added calls. When a patient's turn is short, the transcript alone is not enough to rule out frustration.

##### **Explaining Cross-Label Decision Structure in Multi-Output Tree Ensembles**
2611.20996v1 by Sylvio Barbon Junior, Hiago F. F. Oliveira, Rafael Figueira Gonçalves, Jessica Fernandes Lopes, Ricardo Cerri

A multi-output tree ensemble predicts several binary labels through decision paths that it largely shares across those labels, yet the attribution methods commonly used to explain such models treat each output in isolation. We propose multi-sink Decision Predicate Graphs (ms-DPGs), which extend decision predicate graphs with one sink per label: each leaf contributes to the sink of every label its in-bag positive count for that label. On this representation we define Shared Predicate Flow (SPF), the share of a label's decision mass that passes through conditions active for both labels of a pair, and a contracted view of the graph in which label-specific lifts indicate whether a shared condition raises the probability of both labels or of one at the expense of the other. Controlled experiments show that SPF captures shared decision conditions rather than mere feature overlap. On nine multi-label benchmarks, native multi-output forests share substantially more decision structure than Binary Relevance on eight, including five of the six on which the two are at predictive parity, even at equal hyperparameters, whereas per-label TreeSHAP rankings register the change of architecture without showing whether more is shared. Because a native forest routes every label through the same splits, SPF describes the fitted model rather than the data, and on eight of the nine benchmarks it rises when the feature-label signal is removed; a high SPF is therefore not evidence of shared predictive information. ms-DPG offers a global, predicate-level account of how a multi-output ensemble reuses decision conditions across its labels.

##### **Breaking feature collapse in self-supervised time series encoders**
2611.20981v1 by Özgün Turgut, Daniel Rueckert

General-purpose time series encoders promise a single backbone that generalises across tasks and domains; however, they currently do not scale. We trace this down to a critical point of failure: under the predominant masked data modelling supervision, the encoder's output tokens partially collapse. This is the natural consequence of supervising encoders only in the input space, which leaves no constraint against redundant token dimensions in the feature space. We introduce OTISv2, which suppresses the collapse by complementing the reconstruction objective in input space with two objectives in the feature space: a self-distillation loss against an exponential-moving-average teacher, and a KoLeo regulariser. ##### **Experience Rot: Experienced Agents Fail Where Novices Succeed**
2611.20922v1 by Zhen Xu, Hang Yin, Zitao Zhang, Kevin Zhao, Xuefeng Liu

Agents are increasingly built to learn from experience: they keep conversation histories, memory files, retrieved notes and skills, and reuse what worked before. We show that this experience turns against them when their tools change silently. We test a range of models with tools defined as in the Model Context Protocol (MCP). After a silent change, experienced agents fail on most tasks, while agents new to the task mostly succeed. We call this failure Experience Rot. Experienced agents copy their earlier call instead of checking the current interface, even when the tool definition shows the change. Then they report success. Simple remedies fall short: announcing the update barely helps, and pointing the agent to the documentation fails whenever the documentation does not spell out the answer.
