##### **Verify the Verifier: Specification-Grounded LLM Cross-Checking for Bugs That Escape UVM Scoreboards**
2611.26588v1 by Ana E. Sanchez Villalobos, Michael D. Quinn, Aakash Tyagi, Minh D. Luu, David Kebo Houngninou

A passing UVM regression shows the design agrees with the reference model on what the scoreboard checks, not that it meets the specification. Bugs that pass every test yet violate the specification, termed scoreboard escapes, survive mature testbenches. We present Verify the Verifier, to our knowledge the first framework whose LLM-based Anomaly Cross-Checker examines a passing regression's traces against the specification. Of 564 bugs inserted into four OpenTitan IPs, 68 are candidate scoreboard escapes: non-equivalent, exercised by the tests, yet passing. The framework confirmed 41 as scoreboard escapes with assertions failing on the buggy design and proven on the original.

##### **The Geometry of Lies: Knowledge and Persona Directions for Detecting Deception in Large Language Models**
2611.26545v1 by Md. Abdullah Al Zami, Suraiya Shanaz, Mahdi Hasan, Shoaib Ahmed Dipu, Farig Yousuf Sadeque, Md. Sabbir Ahmed

Linear probes are the cheapest LLM lie detectors but often fail to transfer across kinds of lie. Label-free directions from honest and deceptive role-play are untested at 70B scale and on lies about knowledge, where a lie and an honest denial can be identical. We widen a published persona bank with roles that deny knowledge they have, plus honest counterparts. At each model's middle layer the resulting persona direction ranks lies across Liars' Bench better than single-dataset probes on three of its four models; it fails on Qwen, even when Qwen stays in role far more often. On Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct it beats directions from a random persona bank, but its gain over the published direction is within the range of random widenings. On lies about knowledge, persona directions mainly track a reply's wording: where lies and honest denials share their words, our three main contrasts stay inside the random-direction band on Llama and Qwen and reach only its edge on Gemma, though on Mistral one clears it. A knowledge direction contrasts questions the model can answer with ones nobody can, each followed by the same scripted denial. It separates lies from identical honest denials on two models and narrowly on a third, but not on the fourth; its other results partly depend on the benchmark's wording. On the Gender-Secret fine-tune it clears the band by a few hundredths on Llama and fails on Mistral. The sum of the two directions ranks lies better than a supervised probe at the same depth on all four models (on one, both near chance) but is level with the persona direction on Llama's average and behind that probe on held-out lies. More varied role-play questions do not help the persona direction on Liars' Bench.

##### **Backtracking Agent Applications Security Incidents**
2611.26525v1 by Yicheng Cai, Taegyu Kim, Peng Liu

Agent applications are increasingly deployed, yet agent-specific attacks pose real threats to them. Upon a security incident, organizations backtrack to identify the CEP (compromise entry point), remediate the damage, and prevent its recurrence. The main artifacts available for backtracking are trajectory logs, which record agent functionalities, and source code; app(lication)-specific function call logs are often fairly limited. However, trajectory logs are at the agent functionality abstraction layer, while the CEP candidates are function calls at the app code execution layer. Due to this semantic gap, the analyst needs to manually inspect the relevant code for each CEP candidate to infer its corresponding agent functionality. In this work, we present Origin, a backtracking tool for the analyst to submit a small number of queries to identify CEPs that existing tools cannot provide. Our approach poses two challenges: (1) define the intermediate abstraction that captures the mapping relationships between the two semantic layers; (2) determine the region-to-variable mapping for constructing the cross-layer graph defined in the intermediate abstraction. Each region is a specific group of agent functionalities. Origin addresses the challenges by (1) the cross-layer dependency graph (CLDG) that connects region-inferred functions at the semantic level to the variables in the low-level taint propagation subgraph, plus the associated query language, and (2) the region inference algorithm. Case studies on five popular agent applications across eight attack incidents show that an analyst using Origin correctly identifies all the CEPs.
