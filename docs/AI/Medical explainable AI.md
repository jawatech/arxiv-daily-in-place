##### **DeepQuest: From Knowledge Gaps to Scientific Discovery in Alzheimer's Disease**
2611.26343v1 by Chunbo Jiang, Yifei Chen, Beining Wu, Fuyou Mao, Weihong Chen, Enyu Bao, Mingxuan Liu, Feiwei Qin, Qiyuan Tian

Automating experiments does not necessarily advance scientific understanding. Research agents must identify meaningful knowledge gaps, determine what available data can resolve, and assess whether their findings advance existing knowledge. We introduce DeepQuest, a knowledge-gap-driven AI scientist framework for Alzheimer's disease research. DeepQuest transforms unresolved questions from domain reviews into traceable, testable hypotheses and prioritizes them through data feasibility screening and groupwise Elo ranking. It couples iterative experimentation with two-level auditing that separates experimental reliability from knowledge contribution relative to two frozen literature corpora. On OASIS-3, DeepQuest produces 42 feasible hypotheses and outperforms the strongest baselines in 14 of 15 evaluator-dimension comparisons across three language-model reviewers. The full framework achieves an audit-assessed scientific finding rate of 95.24%, outperforming both general-purpose executors and domain-specific research agents. This scientific finding rate remains stable across literature corpora and evaluator model families. Representative findings concern neuropsychiatric symptoms and dementia risk, longitudinal amyloid spatial stability, and neocortical tau-associated clinical impairment, with key associations supported in independent ADNI and AIBL cohorts. These results show how aligning knowledge gaps, data feasibility, and evidence auditing can turn automated research into traceable, evidence-supported scientific discovery. Our source code is available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/DeepQuest-3066/

##### **Verifiable Logic in Medical LLMs: A Critical Review of Agentic Clinical Reasoning Optimization and Neuro-Symbolic Grounding**
2611.25898v1 by Youssef ElNahas, Adel Ammar, Soumaya Chaffar, Wadii Boulila

Clinical large language models are usually judged by whether the final answer is right. Yet a model can reach a fluent, plausible output through invalid reasoning that static benchmarks do not detect, and confident hallucination, causally invalid inference, and spurious demographic shortcuts can pass conventional evaluation. This critical review covers four paradigms in turn: how clinical hallucination arises and why static metrics miss it; the agentic hallucination cascade, in which multi-agent and tool-augmented systems propagate and amplify early reasoning errors; process reward modeling as step-level supervision; and neuro-symbolic grounding, in which formal logic engines, ontology reasoners, and knowledge graphs place verifiable constraints on neural output. Within their encoded domains, systems with symbolic veto layers offer guarantees that hold relative to their encoded knowledge, and they report large gains on the safety measures they use. These results come from single studies, mostly in simulated or small-sample settings, and are not directly comparable with neural baselines. Process reward models remain bounded by the statistics of their training data. We propose a conceptual framework that maps each agentic failure mode to a symbolic intervention point, and we argue that clinical AI safety depends substantially on system architecture. Because current regulation emphasizes performance and does not require verification of reasoning steps, we argue that responsible certification should also audit reasoning trajectories alongside accuracy on static benchmarks.

##### **The Price of an Explanation: What Demanding Verifiable Artifacts Costs, and What It Buys Back**
2611.25896v1 by Edgar A. Duéñez-Guzmán, Eduardo Dueñez, Edgar Said Hernandez Sanchez, Suzanne Sadedin

Generative models produce many candidate solutions but provide no intrinsic signal by which to rank them. One mitigation is to demand a \emph{verifiable artifact}: a program that can be executed against the available evidence. A common concern is that requiring executable verification incurs costs in both solution quality and computational budget. We measure these two costs on the $120$-task ARC-AGI-2 evaluation set. Demanding a verifiable artifact from an LLM reduced the score from $93$ to $68$ of $120$ tasks ($77\%$ to $57\%$).
