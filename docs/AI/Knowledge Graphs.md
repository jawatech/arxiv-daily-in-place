##### **Modeling and Understanding Neural Weights of Humanoid Policies with Diffusion**
2610.20606v1 by Huixuan Cheng, Bike Zhang, Shankar Sastry, Koushil Sreenath

Recent advances in robot learning have driven rapid progress in humanoid control, enabling increasingly agile behaviors in the real world. However, humanoid control networks are typically obtained through conventional training procedures, while comparatively little attention has been given to directly modeling the distribution of their learned weights and generating functional networks in weight space. In this work, we investigate whether high-performing humanoid control networks can be generatively modeled directly through their weights. We adopt a latent diffusion framework that couples a linear autoencoder with a Diffusion Transformer. The autoencoder compresses actor network parameters into a compact latent space, and the Diffusion Transformer models the distribution of these latents. Trained on networks collected from reinforcement learning for the Unitree G1 robot, our approach generates deployable networks that perform various motions in the real world, including walking, dancing, and spin kick. Beyond generation, we further study the learned weight space to better understand the structure captured by these humanoid control networks. We identify a structural principle in the first-layer weights that consistently reflects the robot's kinematic prior. Notably, this kinematic prior emerges under both Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL) training, suggesting that it is not specific to a particular optimization process but may instead reflect common knowledge associated with humanoid embodiment. Together, these results demonstrate the feasibility of generative modeling in humanoid network weight space, while providing initial insights into the information encoded by their learned weights. Additional results are available on our project website: https://humanoid-weight-diffusion.github.io/

##### **Hierarchical Residual Policy Learning for Real-World Mobile Manipulation with Sparse Human Guidance**
2610.20607v1 by Zhefei Gong, Hefeifei Jiang, Zehao Wang, Jiachen Li

Mobile Manipulation (MoMa) is a cornerstone of autonomous robotic systems, yet real-world fine-tuning remains a formidable challenge because of high-dimensional action spaces and intricate coupling between heterogeneous modules. In this paper, we propose HiRe-MoMa (Hierarchical Residual Mobile Manipulation), a systematic framework for stable and sample-efficient real-world fine-tuning of complex MoMa systems. At its core, HiRe-MoMa adopts a Hierarchical Residual Architecture that fine-tunes a frozen base policy. A Policy-Agnostic Residual learns additive corrections atop the base policy, while Hierarchical Decoupling splits the residual into mobile-base and manipulator-arm heads with unidirectional conditioning, mitigating gradient interference and aligning corrections with the platform's physical hierarchy. To improve sample efficiency in data-expensive real-world settings, we propose Expert-Guided Value Alignment, which leverages sparse human interventions with under 20% training coverage to reshape the critic's value landscape, effectively guiding autonomous exploration through expert priors. Extensive real-world experiments on the TIAGo++ platform demonstrate that HiRe-MoMa consistently enhances task performance with only around 30 minutes of online interaction, regardless of the base policy architecture. To our knowledge, this is among the first frameworks to enable efficient on-robot fine-tuning for mobile manipulation in the real world, providing a practical paradigm for deploying complex robotic systems in dynamic, human-centric environments. Further details can be found on our project website: https://hire-moma.github.io/

##### **Price of Anarchy of First-Price Auctions in Agentic Markets**
2610.20587v1 by Shubhendu Trivedi, Jacob Eisenstein, Alekh Agarwal, Ian Gemp, Michael Kaisers, Adam Fisch

An agentic market is a decentralized serving-time market where AI agents bid to claim each user prompt. An agent's bid is based on its expected value for the prompt, which is the expectation of the reward for its response minus its inference costs. First-price auctions (FPAs), in which the highest bidder pays its bid and receives the prompt, are a natural way to allocate prompts in such a market, since they are simple and transparent to run, and do not require central knowledge of private capabilities and costs. However, FPAs are not truthful: participants may bid less than their true value in the hope of a larger profit, and the prompt may then be allocated to someone other than the bidder who values it the most. This welfare loss is quantified by the $\textit{price-of-anarchy}$ (PoA). In this paper, we argue that agentic markets are likely to possess features that reduce the price-of-anarchy. The first is a $\textit{competitive floor}$, which occurs when multiple bidders usually value the prompt above some baseline share of the optimal welfare (e.g., because the best models are often distilled into cheaper alternatives, which perform similarly). We show that competition among them then limits the welfare loss, which can be driven to zero as the floor rises. The second feature is $\textit{near-independence}$ of values after conditioning on the prompt, which occurs when most of the predictable variation in the expected value is determined by the difficulty of the prompt. 2610.17914v1 by Lingyi Long, Congyue Zhao, Xiaoou Li, Yanmin Qian, Tao Huang

Post-hoc linearization is an attractive way to accelerate pretrained Video Diffusion Transformers (Video DiTs), yet existing methods largely treat the quality loss induced by attention replacement as a recovery problem. We show that a more fundamental bottleneck lies upstream: \emph{initialization}. Random initialization discards pretrained knowledge, whereas manual weight reuse transfers it through rigid and incomplete source--target correspondences. We introduce \textbf{LinMorph}, which recasts Video DiT linearization as a learnable softmax-to-linear weight transformation problem. Instead of copying predefined counterparts, LinMorph learns a cross-parameter morphism that lets each target Gated DeltaNet (GDN) parameter draw jointly from pretrained query, key, value, and output projections, including for parameters with no direct softmax counterpart. A single morphism is shared across layers and learned through local teacher alignment, while producing layer-specific initializations from their respective pretrained weights. The resulting weights are then materialized as independent GDN modules for progressive distillation and joint recovery.Since the morphism operates in weight space rather than token space, it can be learned on short, low-resolution clips and directly reused at larger video scales. Across pretrained Video DiTs, LinMorph enables markedly faster recovery and better final generation quality than random initialization and heuristic weight reuse. Results show that efficient linearization depends not only on how a converted model is recovered, but critically on how pretrained knowledge is transferred before recovery. ##### **Cross-Lingual Transfer of Evil Vectors Is Directionally Asymmetric**
2610.17880v1 by Juann Kim, Minku Kang

Persona vectors are linear directions in the residual-stream activations of LLMs that encode behavioral traits and enable fine-grained control over model behavior. Among them, the evil persona vector is particularly relevant to understanding harmful behavior, jailbreak vulnerabilities, and the unlearning of harmful knowledge. Yet whether this vector reflects a genuinely cross-lingual concept, rather than an artifact of its extraction language, remains untested. We conduct a systematic evil-vector transfer study across two high-resource languages (English, Spanish) and two low-resource languages (Swahili, Telugu) on four open-source instruction-tuned LLMs, and find that transfer is directionally asymmetric. Evil vectors are harder to obtain reliably from low-resource languages, and the resulting vectors are largely ineffective at steering, even within their own language. In contrast, vectors extracted from high-resource languages transfer effectively to low-resource inputs while largely preserving general knowledge, as measured by MMLU. Geometric analysis further supports this pattern: evil vectors from the two high-resource languages are closely aligned, whereas low-resource vectors align only weakly with this shared direction. These results suggest that a shared cross-lingual evil direction exists and, once reliably extracted from a high-resource language, can be reused to steer behavior in low-resource languages without additional per-language data collection.
