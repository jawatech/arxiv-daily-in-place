##### **A Comparison of Uncertainty in AI and Dynamical Climate Ensembles**
2611.31064v1 by Jacob B. Landsberg, Devin M. McAfee, M. A. Fernandez, Elizabeth A. Barnes

Climate model ensembles and their uncertainty estimates are essential for evaluating risk and designing climate policy. Yet, in the assessment of newly developed AI climate models, evaluating uncertainty is often secondary to diagnosing mean-state climate biases and trends. In this work, we conduct the first systematic comparison of uncertainty representation in ensembles of state-of-the-art AI and dynamical atmospheric climate models. Examining 2-meter temperature, 500 hPa geopotential height, and precipitable water, we find that the AI ensemble exhibits reduced internal variability and model uncertainty across variables relative to the dynamical ensemble. We show that this dampened variability causes the AI ensemble to produce more limited climate extremes and yields underdispersion relative to ERA5. Under a uniform warming scenario, the AI ensemble shows increased model uncertainty. Dynamical models grapple with their own challenges, exhibiting stronger miscalibration (overdispersion) relative to ERA5 and larger climate biases than the AI models. As AI and dynamical climate models are mutually integrated into operational climate prediction, careful consideration of their differences in uncertainty representations is crucial for grounded assessment of climate risk.

##### **The AMPEL Platform: An Open-Source Project for Developing Hospital-Wide Clinical Decision Support Systems under EU-Regulation**
2611.30990v1 by Alexander Twrdik, Daniel Steinbach, Martin Federbusch, Clemens Malkawi, Maria Schmidt, Toralf Kirsten

As incentives to digitize more and more aspects of medical healthcare are gaining traction, additional medical data is becoming available. A major goal is to use this data to gain further insights, support medical staff, and ultimately improve patient care. This demands sophisticated solutions that can handle current and future vast amounts of data, all without disrupting well-established workflows of care takers. Additionally, the solution must fulfill the documentation and risk-management obligations of the Medical Device Regulation (MDR), which governs whether a device may be used in patient care in Europe. In this context, we introduce the AMPEL platform, an open-source solution for creating clinical decision support systems (CDSS) that fulfil the requirements of the MDR. Centered around a message broker (Kafka), it adopts an event-driven, distributed architecture, allowing for scaling and efficient processing of medical data as it gets generated. Relying on platform-specific events to implicitly describe the platform's interfaces makes it both extensible and agnostic to the hospital information system in use. With these events being defined in the Protocol Buffers format, medically evaluated algorithms can be independently implemented in a variety of programming languages and easily linked to the platform, extending its analytic capabilities as desired. The platform features a basic UI and alert functionality that can be used to gain medical staff's attention and present analysis results in a traceable and explainable fashion. We further show in a use case how the platform was used to build LAMPE, a CDSS developed and operated at University of Leipzig Medical Center. It demonstrates how the platform solution can be used in a complex and already established environment, while acknowledging current limitations and outlining planned developments to overcome these.

##### **TrialAgentBench: Evaluating AI Agents for Clinical-Trial Analysis and Long-Horizon Drug-Development Decisions**
2611.30916v1 by Bradley Segal, William Bolton, Philip Torr, David Clifton, Lei Clifton

Clinical drug development is a long-horizon, high-stakes process requiring sequential decisions based on information that costs money and time to acquire. AI agents have demonstrated capabilities for scientific analysis, but there is no established way to evaluate them on these decisions, as real trials are expensive, involve people, and never reveal the counterfactual. We introduce TrialAgentBench, a benchmark for evaluating AI agents on clinical trial analysis and drug development decision-making using simulated trials where the truth is known, embedded within the data records of a fictional drug sponsor. We publicly release nearly 3,500 tasks, varying from analysing a single trial to managing multi-phase programmes with budget and time constraints. We score each analysis by rerunning its code on new trials, and each programme by the value its decisions achieve. Across 24 closed- and open-weight models, the strongest agents can analyse raw, messy trial records to within 6% of a process-aware analysis, even when an assumption is violated, with 95% confidence intervals containing the true effect 94--95% of the time.
