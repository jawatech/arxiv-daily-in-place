##### **MemOnce: A Scalable Memory Architecture for Large Language Models**
2611.27973v1 by Chang Nie, Chaoyou Fu, Songlin Liu, Yiqing Hu, Caifeng Shan

Deploying large language models (LLMs) in long-horizon and continual interactions requires maintaining reusable memory states for future inference, posing challenges for existing architectures. Standard Transformers accumulate layer-wise, token-level KV caches, incurring linear GPU memory growth and quadratic computational complexity as the context expands. In contrast, linear-time architectures compress history into fixed-size states, but struggle to retain fine-grained details. We introduce MemOnce, a foundation architecture that learns scalable memory states for language models. MemOnce is based on two core designs: (a) Memory encoder-decoder: it caches only a small subset of final-layer hidden states as compact memory representations and dynamically decodes them back into KV caches for attention layers; (b) Memory sparse attention: for each continual input, MemOnce adaptively retrieves relevant memory representations, with the sparse indices computed only once across layers. Coupling these two designs enables efficient on-the-fly memory loading and KV cache decoding, drastically reducing the GPU memory footprint and achieving linear-time complexity. We conduct extensive experiments to show that MemOnce outperforms Transformers and other architectures across varying model sizes, context lengths, and training paradigms (pretraining and post-training adaptation). In particular, adapting Qwen3.5-4B with MemOnce reduces memory state cache by 1313$\times$ and prefill latency by 6.9$\times$ at 1M context, and improves throughput by 3.5$\times$ and overall memory performance by +5.77%. Finally, MemOnce generalizes zero-shot to downstream representation tasks, achieving competitive results against specialized models.

##### **Non-penetrating World Models**
2611.27963v1 by Yuhang Dai, Yinwei Wu, Ziyue Lin, Han Ji, Xingyi Yang

We study a new problem of preventing object penetration in interactive video world models. To understand how existing models handle this, we introduce NoPeBench, a benchmark that evaluates how world models respond to physical obstacles. Our evaluation reveals that none of the open-source models can reliably prevent such failures. Surprisingly, however, these models already encode the information needed to predict such violations. We find that the hidden states of diffusion transformers (DiTs) can predict whether a candidate action will penetrate an obstacle. Building on this, we introduce NoPeWorld, a lightweight framework that detects and prevents penetration. Specifically, we train a small prediction head on top of a frozen DiT's hidden states to read out if a candidate action is safe or not. Unsafe actions are then replaced with safe ones that best preserve the original intent. Despite its simplicity, NoPeWorld substantially reduces the physical violation rate, averaged over six video world models, from 74% to 16%. Remarkably, our method shows zero-shot generalization to open-domain imagery, third-person viewpoints, and moving obstacles. We publicly release our code, the NoPeBench benchmark, and model weights. Project Page: https://nopeworld.github.io .

##### **Model Carving: Training Transformers That Optimize Their Own Topology**
2611.27962v1 by Kaden Zheng, Pratyusha Sharma

Machine learning has largely kept separate the choice of architecture and the process of training. A classic Transformer, for instance, trains a fixed set of attention and MLP sublayers at every layer, in the same order, and optimizes the weights within. This uniformity is a convention rather than a principled design choice, and it is likely suboptimal: the best architecture for a given task may be smaller, more efficient, and far from uniform. We believe that, ideally, training itself should shape the model's topology. To this end, we introduce Model Carving, which learns both a Transformer's weights and its architecture in a single run. By applying learnable, scalar gates to each sublayer, we allow the training loss to remove the model's own components during training -- freeing a smaller heterogeneous network inside. We evaluate Model Carving by pre-training standard Transformers from 140M to 2.7B parameters and looped Transformers from 140M to 1.3B. At equal training compute, carved models reach a lower validation loss than dense models trained with up to 1.7 times more compute. Furthermore, carved models also outperform size-matched models produced by pruning or distillation, suggesting that the best way to obtain small models for edge devices is to carve them during training, not to compress a large one afterwards.
